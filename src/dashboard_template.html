Trickle-Down Trend Tracker

Can news coverage tell a fashion brand what to stock, and when?

The problem

A brand decides what to make, and how much, months before anyone buys it. Guess wrong and it is left with racks of unsold stock, or sold-out bestsellers it can't restock in time.

The question

Fashion's old rule: trends start on luxury runways, semi-luxury brands adapt them, then high-street brands mass-produce them. If that order is reliable, a brand can see demand coming. Is it? And can press buzz work as an early warning?

The answer so far

Press data is real: GDELT news article counts only (no article text), measured as each trend's share of a tier's brand coverage. Retail data is simulated for a fictional store, and labelled wherever it appears.

What we found

Watchlist: what's rising after the shows

Press momentum, not a sales forecast. Rising = at least 1.5× the trend's pre-show share. Each spring trend comes from runway reporting that names the houses that showed it (linked); candidates without named houses were left out.

In season now: what to market, and when

Press momentum is real. The delay to demand was measured on the simulated store, so the demand window shows how the plan works, not a forecast for any real store; a real store would use the delay measured from its own searches.

3Launch timing, weeks ahead simulated delay

Shoppers follow press with a delay, and the delay comes from what the method measured for each product, never a fixed number. When a trend's semi-luxury press rises, demand is due that many weeks later: start marketing two weeks before, have stock in store one week before. That is the "In season now" panel above.

Harder versions of the test, run on every change

  • A different hidden delay per product (2, 5 and 9 weeks): each must be found. A method that always answered the same number would fail.
  • A tiny, noisy store (about two sales a week): it should mostly say "can't tell" rather than guess, and every product it is confident about must have the right delay.
  • No relationship at all (demand secretly follows unrelated data): it may claim a delay for no more than 5% of products.
